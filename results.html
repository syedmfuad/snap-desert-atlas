A monitoring measure, not evidence of unmet need: census tracts where at least 15% of households receive SNAP but no full-service SNAP-authorized store (supermarket, large grocery, or super store) sits within USDA's food desert distances of half a mile in urban tracts or ten miles in rural ones. The distance thresholds are USDA's; the 15% participation cutoff is this study's own, since USDA screens need by income. Contiguous 48 states plus DC; Alaska and Hawaii reported separately; Puerto Rico excluded because it operates NAP rather than SNAP.

Against USDA's new SNAP retailer map

In July 2026 the Economic Research Service added a SNAP-authorized Retailer Access Map (SRAM) to the Food Access Research Atlas. It counts every SNAP store except farmers markets, so a convenience store satisfies it, and it screens tracts on poverty or median family income rather than SNAP participation. With date, locations, and rule held fixed, 79% of SNAP deserts have a SNAP store of another format within the threshold.

That controlled comparison changes only the store universe. Under USDA's own population-share rule on block-group centers the figure is 63% to 70%. SRAM's published flag rates 6,698 of the 10,836 matched deserts as not low access, and 6,413 of those are also not low access under our any-format rule, so format explains almost all of that gap. Aggregation still matters for which tracts any map flags: our any-format flag agrees with SRAM's in 74% of tracts under the center rule and 91% under the grid rule with SRAM's urban flag. A six-month retailer vintage gap changes 0.5% of flags. The grid rule designates 13,910 SNAP deserts, against 10,836 under the center rule. A further 2,033 desert tracts fail SRAM's low-income screen.

Tribal areas: 235 of the 1,373 tracts that lie mostly inside tribal areas are SNAP deserts (17.1% versus 13.0% elsewhere), holding 72,816 SNAP households.

Food deserts and the participation criterion

Food deserts get the policy attention. The test of whether that attention reaches SNAP households is whether the two designations select the same tracts. Here the access measure is held identical for both, no full-service SNAP store within USDA's food desert distances of half a mile in urban tracts or ten miles in rural ones, and only the need criterion changes: USDA's low-income rule (poverty of 20% or more, or median family income at or below 80% of the state or metro median) against SNAP participation of 15% or more.

2,033 SNAP desert tracts fail USDA's income test, but only 339 of them are robust to survey error and at least 74% already qualify for food financing.

Those tracts average a poverty rate of 13% and a median family income of 104% of the state median, yet 21% of their households receive SNAP. They are 94% urban and 26% Hispanic, and most sit in the Moderate tier. The reverse group, food deserts without a SNAP access problem, is larger: 5,206 tracts where SNAP participation averages 9% and 30% of adults hold a bachelor's degree. Applying the one-mile urban threshold that USDA's atlas uses by default to both measures leaves 737 SNAP desert tracts (19.9%) outside the food desert designation. Blind-spot tracts concentrate in FL 292, CA 219, TX 193, IL 113, PA 95, MI 81, GA 75, OH 74, NY 67, OR 62. Summing probabilities over low-access tracts gives an expected 11,033 deserts, so the national count is stable, but only 47% of designated tracts clear the 15% cutoff at the 90% level. The cutoff also leaves out 2,475,872 SNAP households who live in low-access tracts with participation under 15%.

Cars change the map

The distance thresholds assume households drive. Two vehicle-aware measures test that. A carless core keeps the main definition and asks where at least a fifth of households have no vehicle. A vehicle variant applies USDA's own vehicle-access rule to SNAP need: at least 15% of households on SNAP and either 100 or more carless households beyond half a mile of a full-service store at any density, or a center more than 20 miles from one.

Extensions

Weighted by SNAP households, tract centers are 1.49 miles from a full-service SNAP store: 0.68 in urban tracts, 3.46 in rural ones.

A continuous measure ranks states differently from the binary flag. New Mexico leads on both, but the rest of the top ten by SNAP-household-weighted distance are rural states, and across states the rank correlation between the desert share of tracts and weighted miles is -0.29. The half-mile rule finds urban neighborhoods with no supermarket in walking range; the continuous measure finds the states where miles accumulate. Dollar stores and full-service exit

High-participation tracts draw three times as many new dollar-store authorizations as other tracts. With the sample and controls fixed before the entry window, each new dollar store within a mile is associated with 1.7 percentage points more full-service authorization endings (95% CI 1.1 to 2.3), about 9% of the mean. Tract-level loss of access is too imprecisely estimated to interpret (0.37 points, CI -0.41 to 1.15). These are conditional associations, not effects.

State cost sharing under the 2025 reconciliation law

From fiscal 2028 states pay a share of benefit costs set by their payment error rate. Applying FY2024 rates, the top tier holds 54.8% of the SNAP households living in deserts and 57.5% of all SNAP households, so desert households are not disproportionately exposed. Five jurisdictions combine the top tier with more than 30% of their SNAP households in deserts: CT, DC, FL, NM, OK. How states will respond is not something the map can say.

Methods notes

  • Retailers: USDA Historical SNAP Retailer Locator, 12/31/2024 release. A record is active on a date if authorized on or before it and not yet ended. The 29% of records without coordinates are almost entirely closures before 2006.
  • Full-service means the USDA store types Supermarket, Large Grocery Store, and Super Store. Medium and small grocery, combination grocery/other (mostly dollar stores), convenience, and specialty stores are excluded from the access measure.
  • Urban tracts have ACS population density of at least 1,000 per square mile; SRAM instead uses Census urban areas.
  • Distance is great-circle from the Census population-weighted tract center to the nearest store. Driving distance is not computed here; SRAM's driving-distance flags are used where cited.
  • Tract-level exit risk multiplies store-level probabilities as if exits were independent, which understates risk where closures are correlated.
  • Pipeline: national/ folder, scripts 01 through 07; every figure on this page is generated from outputs/dashboard_data.json.
